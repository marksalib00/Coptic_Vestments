Vestments of the
Priest

Egyptian Festival · Sanctuary Tour
These are the vestments worn by a Coptic Orthodox priest during the Divine Liturgy. Each piece has a name, a history, and a meaning. Read through them as you stand before the display.
Why White?

From the earliest Coptic canonical sources — documents going back to the 4th and 5th centuries — white was required for all clergy serving the liturgy. The reason is scriptural: white is the color of God's own garment in Daniel, of Christ at the Transfiguration, of the angels at the Resurrection, and of the saints in the Book of Revelation. It is also the color of the baptismal garment — the white robe placed on every newly baptized Christian. When a priest vests in white, he is putting on the same garment he received at his baptism, a reminder that he serves not by his own identity, but by the new life he received in Christ.

The Vestments
01
The Tonia طونية
Greek: Sticharion (στιχάριον)
History Meaning

The Tonia is the long white robe worn as the base layer underneath everything else. It is the oldest vestment in the tradition, appearing in the earliest records of Coptic liturgical practice. Every rank — deacon, priest, and bishop — wears it. It is the one garment that belongs to all.

As the Coptic scholar Fr. Tadros Malaty writes, the priest enters the sanctuary in this white vestment "in order to be distinguished not only from others but also even from ourselves, from our ego — that we may be hidden completely in Jesus Christ." The garment is not decoration. It is a act of self-emptying before God.

02
The Sadra صدرة
Greek: Epitrachelion (ἐπιτραχήλιον)
History Meaning

The Sadra is the priestly stole — worn around the neck with both ends hanging straight down the front. Historically, the priestly stole and the deacon's stole began as the same garment, distinguished only by how each rank wore it. Over time they became distinct pieces. The Sadra is the mark of the priestly office, present in the oldest Coptic ordination manuscripts we have.

It represents the grace of the priesthood and calls to mind the image of the shepherd in Luke 15 who finds his lost sheep and lifts it upon his shoulders. The priest, as father and shepherd of his people, carries that same responsibility — and the Sadra sitting on his shoulders is its visible sign.

03
The Bornos برنس
Greek: Phelonion (φελόνιον)
History Meaning

The Bornos is the large outer robe worn over everything else. It became part of standard priestly vestments around the 7th to 8th centuries and is worn primarily on major feast days — the Nativity, the Resurrection, Pentecost, and others.

The prayer recited when the Bornos is placed on a new Patriarch captures its meaning: "My soul rejoices in the Lord, for He has clothed me with the garment of salvation and vested me with a robe of gladness." It is the garment of joy — the outer expression of what the liturgy itself celebrates: the gift of salvation.

04
The Taylassan طيلسان
Persian origin — no Greek equivalent
History Meaning

The Taylassan is the pointed head covering worn by the priest. Historically, priests served bareheaded in the early church. Head coverings began among monastic priests in the Egyptian desert before the 8th century and gradually became standard for all clergy by the 13th century. The word itself comes from a Persian term for a secular head covering that was adopted into church use over time.

Theologically it represents the crown of the Kingdom — as Christ was enthroned as King, so the priest who represents Christ at the altar wears a crown. Some priests remove it during the Epiclesis — the moment the Holy Spirit is invoked over the bread and wine — as an act of humility, acknowledging that Christ present on the altar outranks the one celebrating.

05
The Zinnar زنار
Greek: Zone (ζώνη)
History Meaning

The Zinnar is the belt tied around the waist over the other vestments. It first appears in Coptic sources in the 13th century. Practically, it holds the stole in place during the many movements of the liturgy — censing, baptisms, and prostrations.

Spiritually, it represents readiness for service. Saint Paul writes in Ephesians 6:14: "Stand therefore, having girded your waist with truth." The belt is the priest physically preparing himself — tying himself into a posture of readiness before God.

06
The Komam أكمام
Greek: Epimanikia (ἐπιμανίκια)
History Meaning

The Komam are the cuffs worn around the wrists. Like the belt, they appear in Coptic sources from the 13th century onward. There is a uniquely Coptic tradition about their origin: they began as handkerchiefs the priest carried during the liturgy to wipe his tears in prayer, and over time became formalized as vestments.

Theologically, they represent the hands of the priest becoming the hands of Christ — it is Christ who distributes the mysteries to the congregation through the priest. During the Institution Narrative of the Liturgy of Saint Basil, the priest prays quietly: "Exchange my hands for your hands." The cuffs are a reminder of exactly that exchange.

Some of these vestments go back to the very beginning of the church. Others were added centuries later. Some have faded from regular use. But the core has remained — the white robe, the stole, the outer garment — present in every Coptic liturgy, in every country, for nearly two thousand years.

"The congregation is to see less of the priest and more of Christ — the true High Priest, present at every liturgy."
— Kyrillos Tadros, Vestments of Glory and Honor (2019)
